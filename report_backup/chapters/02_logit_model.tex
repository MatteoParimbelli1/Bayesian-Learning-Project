\section{Logit model}
\label{sec:logit}

In this chapter we present the results obtained by fitting a simple Bayesian
logistic regression on a reduced set of covariates, in order to establish a
first, fully interpretable baseline solution and to settle a few modelling
choices (prior specification, class encoding, MCMC setup) reused by the
shrinkage model of Section~\ref{sec:regression}.

\subsection{Covariates and prior specification}
Following the suggestions of the brief, we fit the model on a compact subset of
covariates, using the transformations motivated in the data-exploration phase
(Section \ref{sec:data_expl}): \texttt{age}, \texttt{seat\_comfort},
\texttt{flight\_distance}, \texttt{class}, the log-transformed departure
delay $\log(1+\texttt{dep\_delay})$ and the \texttt{signed-log mid-flight delay}, together
with the two binary descriptors \texttt{travel\_type} and
\texttt{customer\_type}. All continuous covariates are standardized
(zero mean, unit variance) so that the common prior variance is equally
informative for every covariate. For the binary covariates the reference
level is chosen as the majority group (\textit{Business travel} and
\textit{Loyal customer}).
The model \ref{eq:logit_model} is still the same, just reduced at these variables.

\paragraph{Prior specification.}
Since we have no strong prior belief on the magnitude or the sign of the
coefficients, and because standardisation already puts every covariate on a
comparable scale, we adopt independent, weakly informative Gaussian priors on
all the parameters:
\begin{equation}
    \beta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 100), \qquad
    \beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \;\; (j = 1,\dots,P), \qquad
    \beta_{\text{mid}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 100).
    \label{eq:priors}
\end{equation}
Here the second argument denotes the prior \emph{variance} $\sigma^2 = 100$
(equivalently a precision $1/\sigma^2 = 0.01$ in the JAGS
parametrisation). On the logit scale a standard deviation of $10$ is very
diffuse: it places essentially no informative constraint on plausible
coefficient values while still being proper, so the posterior is driven mostly
by the likelihood.

\subsection{Encoding of the flight class}
Before committing to the model we address a small preliminary question: is it
preferable to encode \texttt{class} as a pair of dummy variables
(\textit{Eco Plus} and \textit{Business}, with \textit{Eco} as reference) or as a
single centred ordinal score ($1/2/3$)? To decide, we fit the model
\eqref{eq:priors} under both encodings and compare them with the Widely
Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC) \cite{bl_book}, computed from the pointwise
log-likelihood of the MCMC draws.

The dummy encoding attains the lower WAIC ($1373.4$ against $1379.1$ for the
ordinal one), i.e. a slightly better expected predictive fit. The difference in
expected log predictive density is $\Delta\text{elpd} = -3$ with a standard
error of $2.7$, so the preference, although real, is smaller than two standard
errors and therefore modest. Because \texttt{class} is genuinely
\emph{nominal} rather than an equally-spaced ordinal scale, and because the
dummy specification is both more flexible and lets us report a separate,
directly interpretable odds ratio for each class, we adopt the dummy encoding
for the rest of the analysis. To verify that this in-sample preference is not an artefact of the modest
training size ($n=1213$), we re-score both encodings on the held-out test
set ($n\approx128{,}000$ customers), using the posterior-mean predictive
probability $\hat p_i = S^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\left(\hat\beta_0^{(s)} + x_i\hat\beta^{(s)}\right)$
obtained from the already-fitted MCMC draws (without additional sampling). The dummy encoding again outperforms the ordinal one on all three
out-of-sample scoring rules considered: log-loss (0.557 against 0.562),
Brier score (0.187 against 0.190), and AUC (0.786 against 0.781).

Four effects clearly separate from the ``no-effect'' line (OR $=1$). The
largest in magnitude is customer loyalty, and it runs negative: a
\textit{disloyal customer} has markedly lower odds of being satisfied. Cabin
class comes next: travelling in \textit{Business} rather than \textit{Eco}
multiplies the odds by roughly four, even after adjusting for all the other
covariates. \textit{Seat comfort} follows: more comfortable seating is
strongly associated with satisfaction. A longer departure delay reduces the
odds too.

The remaining covariates have credible intervals that comfortably include $1$
and cannot be called confident drivers on their own: \textit{Eco Plus} is
statistically indistinguishable from \textit{Eco}, while \textit{age},
\textit{flight distance} and the \textit{mid-flight delay} all show
weak, uncertain effects.
