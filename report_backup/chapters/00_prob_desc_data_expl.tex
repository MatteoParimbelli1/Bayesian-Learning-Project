\section{Problem description and data exploration}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Problem description}
The goal of this project is to predict the level of customer satisfaction of an unspecified airline, given a set of covariates $X$ that describe several characteristics of the flight, such as service quality items, type of customer, departure delay and so on, and customer's opinion on the flight, like the rating given to some service quality items. Given the high degree of uncertainty inherent in personal opinions, we chose to adopt a Bayesian logistic regression model\cite{bl_book}. 
%improve this
The Bayesian coefficients are estimated via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, sampled in JAGS.

\subsection{Dataset description}

The data set~\cite{kaggle_ds} contains 129{,}880 customer records, described by 22
columns. \texttt{Arrival Delay} is missing for 393 records (0.3\% of the data);
given the negligible share, we drop these rows rather than impute them, leaving
129{,}487 clean records. Of these, a stratified subsample of 1213 rows is used for
model fitting (Section~\ref{sec:data_expl}), and the remaining 128{,}274 records are held out for
out-of-sample validation (Section~\ref{sec:prediction}).

The columns are organized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Target variable}: represents the satisfaction level of customer $i$,
denoted $y_i$, which can be either \emph{dissatisfied} or \emph{satisfied} and is
encoded respectively as $\{0,1\}$.

\item \textbf{Categorical variables (3)}: indicate the category of the customer
and of the flight to which the record refers. For these variables, one-hot
encoding is used. Variable list: \texttt{Customer type}, \texttt{Type of Travel},
\texttt{Class}.

\item \textbf{Discrete variables (15)}: mostly composed of quality-of-service
variables. Variable list (service-quality items excluded): \texttt{Age}.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Service-quality variables (14)}: indicate the various levels of
  service available throughout the whole journey, from booking to arrival. These
  variables contain several zeros, which may indicate that the corresponding
  service was not available during the flight. Variable list: \texttt{Seat
  comfort}, \texttt{Departure/Arrival time convenience}, \texttt{Food and drink},
  \texttt{Gate location}, \texttt{Inflight wifi service}, \texttt{Inflight
  entertainment}, \texttt{Online support}, \texttt{Ease of Online booking},
  \texttt{On-board service}, \texttt{Leg room service}, \texttt{Baggage handling},
  \texttt{Check-in service}, \texttt{Cleanliness}, \texttt{Online boarding}.
  \end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Flight properties (3)}: report the spatial and temporal properties
of the flight. They are treated as continuous variables. Variable list:
\texttt{Flight distance}, \texttt{Departure delay}, \texttt{Arrival delay}.

\item \textbf{Modified variables}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item owing to the extremely high correlation between \texttt{Departure delay}
  and \texttt{Arrival delay}, we decided to replace the latter with the
  difference between the two delays, so as to retain the information while
  reducing the correlation.
  \item given the high skewness of variables \texttt{Departure Delay} and
  \texttt{Mid-flight delay}, we decided to correct it through a logarithm
  transformation. \texttt{Flight Distance} was tested but kept on its original
  scale as transforming it worsened rather than improving the prediction.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Further details on missing-data handling and the delay transformations are given
in Appendix~\ref{app:data_aug}.

\subsection{Dataset exploration}
\label{sec:data_expl}
We begin our exploration by examining the balance of the target variable for the
prediction model. The two classes are close to even, $54.7\%$ satisfied
against $45.3\%$ dissatisfied (Tab.~\ref{tab:class_balance}), so the use of
models more complex than a Bernoulli probability distribution is not justified
and will not be pursued.

To obtain a first indication of the relationship between the response variable and the remaining covariates, we examine their correlation, as shown in Fig.~\ref{img:full_corr}. Focusing on the service-quality variables (Fig.~\ref{img:qs_corr}), we can distinguish three subgroups of features. The correlation is computed using R's \texttt{corr()} function, which is based on Pearson's correlation coefficient
$$
r_{X,Y} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}
               {\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
$$
Tab.~\ref{tab:class_balance} reports the class balance of the remaining categorical variables. Given the class imbalance of some covariates, we decided to stratify the train--test split of the dataset.
