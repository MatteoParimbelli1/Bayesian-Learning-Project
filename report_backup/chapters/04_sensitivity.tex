\section{Prior Sensitivity and Structural Robustness}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
This section stress-tests the conclusions of Section~\ref{sec:logit}
(vague-prior logit) and Section~\ref{sec:regression} (horseshoe) against the
modelling choices we made along the way. Each of the four checks below opens
with the question it answers. The chapter then closes by comparing the two
fitted models directly via WAIC and PSIS-LOO \cite{bl_book}, which formalises
the choice of carrying the horseshoe model forward to
Section~\ref{sec:prediction}.

% ==================================================================
\subsection{Horseshoe prior sensitivity: does the global scale matter?}
\label{sec:sens-horseshoe}
% ==================================================================
The horseshoe's shrinkage behaviour is governed by the scale $s$ of the
half-Cauchy prior placed on the \textbf{global} scale $\tau$:
\[
  \tau \sim \mathcal{C}^+(0, s).
\]
Small $s$ pulls every coefficient hard towards zero (more aggressive
sparsity); large $s$ relaxes the global shrinkage. This is the parameter
whose choice could plausibly change \emph{which} covariates the model
keeps, so it is the one worth testing.

We refit the Section~\ref{sec:regression} model
three times, varying only this global scale,
$s \in \{0.1,\, 1,\, 10\}$ ($s = 1$ is the Section~\ref{sec:regression} default), while the
local scales $\lambda_j$ keep the standard $\mathcal{C}^+(0,1)$. For each
fit it stores the posterior mean and $95\%$ credible interval of every
coefficient, and the per-predictor shrinkage weight
\[
  \kappa_j = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda_j^2}
\]
(near $0$ = signal, near $1$ = shrunk, exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:regression}).

\subsubsection{Shrinkage weights and signal/noise stability}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
Turning each $\kappa_j$ into a verdict ($\kappa_j < 0.5 \Rightarrow$ signal)
lets us ask the sharper question: does the global scale change \emph{which}
covariates the model keeps? Across a hundred-fold range of $s$, two of the
$22$ predictors change their classification: \texttt{Online support} and
\texttt{Cleanliness}, both of which sit within $0.02$ of the $\kappa = 0.5$
boundary at the default scale and so flip on the smallest movement. The other
twenty keep the same verdict throughout. The eleven the model calls signal at
$s = 1$ (\texttt{disloyal Customer}, \texttt{Inflight entertainment},
\texttt{Class: Business}, \texttt{Personal Travel}, \texttt{Seat comfort},
\texttt{On-board service}, \texttt{Ease of Online booking},
\texttt{Dep/Arr time convenient}, \texttt{Checkin service},
\texttt{Leg room service} and \texttt{Food and drink}) are the same eleven
at $s = 0.1$ and at $s = 10$, apart from the two boundary cases.

The strong effects of Section~\ref{sec:regression}
(\texttt{disloyal Customer}, \texttt{Personal Travel}, \texttt{Seat comfort})
stay strong and confidently non-zero regardless of $s$, and the largest shift
in any posterior mean is $0.036$ on the log-odds scale. Only the coefficients
already sitting near the boundary drift, which is exactly where sensitivity to
the global scale is expected and where we drew no firm conclusions in the first
place. The headline reading of Section~\ref{sec:regression} is therefore not an
artefact of the particular half-Cauchy scale we chose. The full
$\kappa_j$ values at each scale, and the verdict table they produce, are in
Appendix~\ref{app:hs_shrinkage}.

\subsection{Structural-zero robustness check}
\label{sec:sens-structzero}\label{sec:robustness}
% ==================================================================
\emph{Does the counter-intuitive \texttt{Departure/Arrival time convenient}
coefficient survive dropping the ambiguous structural-zero rows?}
Section~\ref{sec:regression} speculated that its
counter-intuitive \emph{negative} coefficient might be an artefact of
the structural zeros flagged in Section~\ref{sec:data_expl} (zeros that plausibly mean
``not applicable'' rather than genuine dissatisfaction). We test that
directly by refitting the full horseshoe model with those rows excluded
: $110$ rows are
dropped, leaving $1103$.

Table~\ref{tab:structzero} reports the refit, and Figure~\ref{fig:structzero}
compares the ``seat/catering/ground'' cluster with and without the
ambiguous rows.

\noindent\textbf{The hypothesis does not survive the check.}
\texttt{Departure/Arrival time convenient}'s negative coefficient does
not shrink towards zero when the ambiguous rows are removed; it gets
\emph{more} negative (from $-0.334$ to $-0.506$), while its credible
interval widens slightly, as expected with $110$ fewer rows. Whatever
drives this effect, it is not
simply the structural-zero ambiguity we suspected in Section~\ref{sec:regression}; we
report this as an open, unresolved finding rather than force a tidier
story onto it. On the other hand, the other two members of that same correlated
cluster move in \emph{opposite} directions once the ambiguous rows are
excluded. \texttt{Seat comfort} strengthens substantially ($\kappa$
drops from $0.370$ to $0.215$, $\beta$ rises from $+0.424$ to
$+0.869$), whereas \texttt{Food and drink} goes from a borderline
signal ($\kappa = 0.486$, $\beta = -0.280$) to firmly shrunk
($\kappa = 0.732$, $\beta = +0.025$). The structural zeros therefore
do not add uniform noise across the cluster; they affect its members
differently.

\subsection{Sample-size robustness}
\label{sec:sens-samplesize}
% ==================================================================
\emph{Do any of the conclusions depend on the size of the training set?}
The previous three checks varied the prior and which rows enter the fit; this
one varies how many. We refit the model of Section~\ref{sec:logit} on a
stratified subsample of \num{15003} records, twelve times the \num{1213} used
throughout. This leaves the coefficients we rely on
unmoved (\texttt{Class: Business} by $0.005$, \texttt{disloyal
Customer} by $0.004$, \texttt{Seat comfort} by $0.023$) and contracts
the intervals by a median factor of $3.54$, in line with the $3.52$ that
$\sqrt{n}$ asymptotics predicts. Four weak effects
(\texttt{Flight Distance}, \texttt{Age}, \texttt{Personal Travel},
\texttt{Mid-flight delay}) acquire intervals excluding zero, which is a limitation of the
$n = 1213$ analysis rather than a finding about the airline.

\subsection{Model comparison: WAIC and PSIS-LOO}
\label{WPSIS}

Alongside the WAIC-based comparison of Section~\ref{sec:class-encoding} (used there to choose the
\texttt{Class} encoding), we compare the vague-prior logit of Section~\ref{sec:logit} and
the horseshoe model of Section~\ref{sec:regression} via Pareto-smoothed importance sampling
leave-one-out cross-validation (PSIS-LOO), computed from the same pointwise
log-likelihood draws already collected for each fit.

\begin{table}[h]
\small
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
Model & elpd\_loo (SE) & $p_{\text{loo}}$ & LOO-IC \\
\hline
Vague-prior logit & $-686.7$ (16.7) & 10.4 & 1373.4 \\
Horseshoe          & $-491.8$ (21.2) & 21.8 & 983.7 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{PSIS-LOO comparison between the two models, on the $n=1213$
training set. All Pareto $k$ diagnostics are good ($k<0.7$) for both models,
so the PSIS-LOO estimates are reliable without further caveats.}
\end{table}

The horseshoe attains a substantially higher expected log predictive density
($\Delta\text{elpd} = -194.9$, SE $= 17.5$): the difference is more than eleven
standard errors, a decisive preference by any conventional threshold. This
agrees with the WAIC comparison of Section~\ref{sec:class-encoding} -- indeed the logit's LOO-IC
(1373.4) essentially reproduces its WAIC (1373.2) from Table~2, confirming
the two approximations agree here -- and justifies carrying the horseshoe
model forward as the model of record for Section~\ref{sec:prediction}.
