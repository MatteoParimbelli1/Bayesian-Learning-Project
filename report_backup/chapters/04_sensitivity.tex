% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Prior Sensitivity and Structural Robustness}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
This section stress-tests the conclusions of Section~02 (vague-prior
logit) and Section~03 (horseshoe) against the modelling choices we made
along the way. We ask four questions: does the vague model's posterior
depend on the prior \emph{variance} we picked? Does the horseshoe's
signal/noise verdict depend on the \emph{global scale} of its
half-Cauchy prior? Does the counter-intuitive
\texttt{Departure/Arrival time convenient} coefficient from Section~03
survive dropping the ambiguous structural-zero rows? And do any of the
conclusions depend on the size of the training set?

All of the checks below require heavy JAGS fits, so they are split into
separate script files (\texttt{04a\_prior\_sweep.R},
\texttt{04b\_horseshoe\_prior\_sweep.R}, \texttt{04c\_structural\_zero\_refit.R})
to avoid excessively long cells. Each script is meant to be executed
once and its stored output re-used.

% ==================================================================

% ==================================================================
\subsection{Horseshoe prior sensitivity: does the global scale matter?}
\label{sec:sens-horseshoe}
% ==================================================================
The horseshoe's shrinkage behaviour is governed by the scale $s$ of the
half-Cauchy prior placed on the \textbf{global} scale $\tau$:
\[
  \tau \sim \mathcal{C}^+(0, s).
\]
Small $s$ pulls every coefficient hard towards zero (more aggressive
sparsity); large $s$ relaxes the global shrinkage. This is the parameter
whose choice could plausibly change \emph{which} covariates the model
keeps, so it is the one worth testing.

\texttt{04b\_horseshoe\_prior\_sweep.R} refits the exact Section~03 model
three times, varying only this global scale,
$s \in \{0.1,\, 1,\, 10\}$ ($s = 1$ is the Section~03 default), while the
local scales $\lambda_j$ keep the standard $\mathcal{C}^+(0,1)$. For each
fit it stores the posterior mean and $95\%$ credible interval of every
coefficient, and the per-predictor shrinkage weight
\[
  \kappa_j = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda_j^2}
\]
(near $0$ = signal, near $1$ = shrunk, exactly as in Section~03).

Across a hundred-fold range of the global scale, one of the
$22$ predictors changes its signal/noise classification. The covariates
the model calls signal at the default scale ($s = 1$) ---
\texttt{disloyal Customer}, \texttt{Inflight entertainment},
\texttt{Class: Business}, \texttt{On-board service},
\texttt{Personal Travel}, \texttt{Seat comfort},

The headline reading of Section~03 is therefore not an artefact of the
particular half-Cauchy scale we chose.

\noindent\textbf{The hypothesis does not survive the check.}
\texttt{Departure/Arrival time convenient}'s negative coefficient does
not shrink towards zero when the ambiguous rows are removed --- it gets
\emph{more} negative (from $-0.335$ to $-0.513$), while its credible
interval widens slightly, as expected with $110$ fewer rows. Whatever
drives this effect, it is not
simply the structural-zero ambiguity we suspected in Section~03; we
report this as an open, unresolved finding rather than force a tidier
story onto it. On the other hand, the other two members of that same correlated
cluster move in \emph{opposite} directions once the ambiguous rows are
excluded. \texttt{Seat comfort} strengthens substantially ($\kappa$
drops from $0.367$ to $0.209$, $\beta$ rises from $+0.419$ to
$+0.881$), whereas \texttt{Food and drink} goes from a borderline
signal ($\kappa = 0.492$, $\beta = -0.275$) to firmly shrunk
($\kappa = 0.733$, $\beta = +0.020$). The structural zeros therefore
do not add uniform noise across the cluster; they affect its members
differently.

\subsection{Sample-size robustness}
\label{sec:sens-samplesize}
% ==================================================================
The logit prior-variance sweep (Section~\ref{sec:sens-logit}) and the
horseshoe global-scale sweep (Section~\ref{sec:sens-horseshoe}) varied
the prior; the structural-zero refit
(Section~\ref{sec:sens-structzero}) varied which rows enter the fit.
This one varies how many. Every model in this report is estimated on
\num{1213} records,
about $1\%$ of the cleaned dataset, a choice forced by MCMC cost.
\texttt{04d\_large\_subsample\_refit.R} refits the model of
Section~\ref{sec:logit} on a stratified
subsample of \num{15003} records. This leaves the coefficients we rely on
unmoved (\texttt{Class: Business} by $0.005$, \texttt{disloyal
Customer} by $0.014$, \texttt{Seat comfort} by $0.022$) and contracts
the intervals by a median factor of $3.42$, against the $3.52$ that
$\sqrt{n}$ asymptotics predicts. Three weak effects
(\texttt{Flight Distance}, \texttt{Age}, \texttt{Mid-flight delay})
acquire intervals excluding zero, which is a limitation of the
$n = 1213$ analysis rather than a finding about the airline.

\subsection{Model comparison: WAIC and PSIS-LOO}

Alongside the WAIC-based comparison of Section~2.2 (used there to choose the
\texttt{Class} encoding), we compare the vague-prior logit of Section~2 and
the horseshoe model of Section~3 via Pareto-smoothed importance sampling
leave-one-out cross-validation (PSIS-LOO), computed from the same pointwise
log-likelihood draws already collected for each fit.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
Model & elpd\_loo (SE) & $p_{\text{loo}}$ & LOO-IC \\
\hline
Vague-prior logit & $-686.7$ (16.7) & 10.4 & 1373.4 \\
Horseshoe          & $-491.8$ (21.2) & 21.8 & 983.7 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{PSIS-LOO \cite{vehtari2017loo} comparison between the two models, on the $n=1213$
training set. All Pareto $k$ diagnostics are good ($k<0.7$) for both models,
so the PSIS-LOO estimates are reliable without further caveats.}
\end{table}

The horseshoe attains a substantially higher expected log predictive density
($\Delta\text{elpd} = -194.9$, SE $= 17.5$): the difference is more than eleven
standard errors, a decisive preference by any conventional threshold. This
agrees with the WAIC comparison of Section~2.2 -- indeed the logit's LOO-IC
(1373.4) essentially reproduces its WAIC (1373.2) from Table~2, confirming
the two approximations agree here -- and justifies carrying the horseshoe
model forward as the model of record for Section~5.
